# How to Mix Brass Instruments in a Modern Production

 Brass instruments have two modes: supportive and dominant.

 A brass section in a jazz arrangement provides harmonic support — warm, present, slightly back. A trumpet lead in a funk production is the most aggressive element in the mix. The same instruments, entirely different mix treatment.

 The frequency anatomy of brass 

 Trumpet and flugelhorn: fundamental range 150–1000Hz, overtones and brightness extending to 8kHz and above. The characteristic “bite” of a trumpet is around 3–5kHz.

 Trombone: fundamentals from 80–600Hz, body and warmth at 100–400Hz, presence and attack at 2–4kHz.

 French horn: fundamentals 80–800Hz, warm and round character concentrated in the 200–500Hz range.

 Saxophones (often grouped with brass): fundamentals 80–1200Hz depending on register, characteristic honk at 600Hz–1.5kHz.

 The dynamic range challenge 

 Brass instruments have enormous dynamic range. A trumpet played softly is a fundamentally different sound from a trumpet played at full blast. The timbre changes — a soft trumpet has warm, rounded harmonics; a loud trumpet has bright, aggressive harmonics.

 Heavy compression on brass flattens this dynamic range and with it, the timbral variation. The brass loses its expressiveness and sounds plastic.

 Use compression gently on brass — 2:1 ratio, slow attack (30–50ms), catching only the loudest peaks. The natural dynamics of the performance are part of the instrument’s character.

 EQ for presence and space 

 Brass in a dense mix needs EQ to sit without overwhelming other elements.

 High-pass filter: brass instruments don’t need content below their fundamental range. A trombone doesn’t need content below 80Hz. A trumpet doesn’t need content below 150Hz. High-pass to remove any low-frequency room or rumble contribution.

 The body zone (200–600Hz): brass can accumulate body and density here, creating a broad, boxy presence. A gentle cut of 1–2dB in this range opens the mix without making the brass thin.

 Presence and attack: if the brass sounds distant or lacking definition, a gentle boost at 2–4kHz brings out the attack and articulation. If the brass is too aggressive or harsh, a narrow cut at 3–5kHz reduces the bite without removing warmth.

 Brass section as a group 

 A brass section — multiple horns playing together — benefits from group bus processing. Send all brass to a group bus with a gentle compressor and EQ.

 The bus compressor catches the peaks of the section as a whole, creating a sense that the section breathes together. Individual instruments have their own dynamics; the section compressor creates group cohesion.

 Room and reverb 

 Brass instruments recorded close-mic’d have little natural room. Adding an appropriate room reverb — a live studio or concert hall reverb — places the section in a physical space. The reverb should match the character of the recording: close-mic’d jazz brass benefits from a smaller, more intimate room than orchestral brass.

 For modern pop and funk productions with sampled or synthesized brass, a chamber reverb with a short pre-delay (15–20ms) creates the sense of the instrument being in a space without becoming washy.

 Panning brass sections 

 A brass section is traditionally spread across the stereo field: lead trumpet at center or slightly left, second trumpet right, trombone left or right, baritone sax opposite. This creates a natural stereo image that mirrors how a jazz ensemble sits on stage.

 In a modern production context, the brass can be panned more symmetrically — centered for punch, or spread to ±20–30 for width — depending on whether the brass is leading or supporting.
